1. The structure in thirty seconds

You prepay once. For the first six years the equipment sits on the books of HDM, the finance company — and that custody window is what lets HDM claim the 30% federal commercial credit (§48E) that owned residential systems lost when §25D ended for 2026 installs. The credit is monetized into your price on day one. The six years isn’t arbitrary, either: the agreement is built to give the finance company the full time federal rules allow to capture the credit — and then hand it over. At year six, ownership transfers to you at your request — the agreements are built around you making it (the notice windows are laid out in §4), and from that day the system isn’t just on your property; it’s yours.

And every warranty makes the trip with it. The installer’s 25-year workmanship warranty (and roof-penetration warranty, on roof installs) continues with you after the transfer. The equipment makers’ warranties — the panel manufacturer’s product and performance warranties, Tesla’s Powerwall warranty — automatically assign to you the day you take ownership. That’s not a promise, it’s a clause: PPA §10(e), in HDM’s own words — “all workmanship and equipment manufacturer warranties included with the Solar System will automatically be assigned to you” — matched by the installer’s written commitment that “all warranties will continue to apply.” The ownership structure doesn’t shorten any of the warranties you’d have had owning the system outright from day one — same makers, same terms, same clocks.

Ownership transfer at no cost — in writing. It eliminates any concern over the “fair market value” (FMV) verbiage: HDM’s paper prices the year-6 purchase at fair market value. But the same clause already protects you from that price tag: on a prepaid agreement, every year of power you’ve paid for and not yet received is credited against the price — wherever the fair market value lands, the prepaid credit comes off the top, and at year six that credit is roughly fourteen years of production (the math is in §4). Then, even though the design doesn’t need it, one more layer is documented for good measure: the installer’s written guarantee, inside the installation agreement you sign with them, that the transfer happens at no cost to you. The two agreements work as a pair, by design: HDM’s grants the transfer right and carries the credit that covers it; the installer’s guarantees the outcome — each signed by the party it binds. If a rep tells you “free at year six,” ask to see both in writing — that’s where it lives, and where ours is — see the executed example.

2. The ownership clauses that sound scary

The clause What it says What it actually does
“You will have no ownership interest in the System” — PPA §2(b)(i) You don’t own it yet — HDM does, until your year-6 transfer True, and temporary — the same sentence you’d find in any financed-equipment contract. The year-6 transfer makes it permanently false.
UCC-1 / fixture filing — PPA §2(b)(ii), ESSA §2(b)(iii) HDM files a public notice on the equipment The agreement says in its own words this is not a lien on your home. It’s notice that the equipment belongs to the finance company. Title to your home stays clean — the filing follows the equipment, not the property (§6 covers selling). If a lender ever asks about it during a refinance, it’s the same routine filing they see on financed HVAC and water heaters every day. Once you own the equipment, it has no further purpose.
No selling, moving, altering, disconnecting the system — PPA §2(b)(ii) Don’t modify what you don’t own yet Standard for financed equipment; falls away at purchase. Need roof work? §14(d): HDM does the move or approves your contractor.
Limited power of attorney — PPA §8(a) HDM can sign utility paperwork for you Strictly limited to net-metering and interconnection filings. A convenience so you don’t chase utility forms — not control over anything else.
“All federal tax credits, incentives, RECs and rebates are the property of HDM” — PPA §8(e), ESSA §6 HDM keeps the incentives Correct — and it’s the machine producing your discount. The same section states your price and payments reflect those incentives. You receive the credit’s value as a lower price on day one. The credit is claimed on HDM’s return, not yours — there’s nothing on your return to audit or claw back.

3. The money — the complete cost picture

The honest list — built to include everything, and checkable: if you or your AI find a cost in the agreements this list missed, send it over and we’ll answer it against the clause. A prepaid agreement has $0.00 monthly payments and a 0.0% escalator, printed on the cover page. Beyond the prepayment itself:

  1. Annual administration fee — only if you don’t take ownership. PPA §11(a): system kW × $48.00/year; ESSA §9(a): battery kWh × $18.00/year; both escalate 2.9% annually and both bill only “if you do not elect to purchase… pursuant to Section 10(a)(i) / 8(a)(i).” The first six years are already inside your prepayment. Left unexercised, the fee runs annually until purchase or the end of the extension periods (PPA §13(c)) — a tail that can run well past a decade. That’s not a trap; it’s the contract telling you what it’s built for: everyone is expected to take the transfer at year six, and when you do, this fee never bills once.
  2. $250 agreement transfer fee — PPA §12(a) / ESSA §10(a): applies if the agreement is assigned to a new homeowner (i.e., you sell before taking ownership). No credit check, no minimum FICO for the buyer.
  3. A second administration fee if the battery manufacturer’s warranty is voided by unauthorized use — ESSA §9(b), capped at the remaining prepayment value. Avoided by using the system normally.
  4. Late fees on unpaid balances (1.5%/month on amounts past due) — moot on a prepaid deal with nothing billing monthly.
  5. Homeowner’s insurance — PPA §15(b) / ESSA §13(b): you confirm your existing policy covers the system. This is your current policy, not a new product. (HDM carries its own insurance too — §15(a)/§13(a).)
  6. Personal property taxes, if your jurisdiction levies them — §2(b)(iii); and applicable taxes on the year-6 purchase price (which the installer guarantee addresses — §4).
  7. Your regular utility relationship continues — PPA §8(a),(c): you keep net-metering service, pay the utility’s basic connection charge, and buy any shortfall the system doesn’t produce. Solar reduces the bill; no honest company says it eliminates the account.
  8. System removal at end of term: $0 (disclosure Box C) — and never happens if you own it.

Payment timing: nothing is due to HDM until the work happens — the solar prepayment on or before system start, the storage prepayment at commencement of installation (ESSA §7(a)). There is no down-payment-at-signing structure to worry about.

4. Year six — the transfer, the math, and the timeline

The option (PPA §10(a)): you may purchase within 60 days following the sixth anniversary — also when 75% or 100% of your prepaid output has been delivered, or as early as 30 days before selling your home (never earlier than the sixth anniversary).

The price (PPA §10(c)): fair market value — federal law requires an FMV option on this structure (IRC §7701(e)(4)(A)(iv); a fixed bargain price would break the very safe harbor that produces the 30% discount). But the same section then applies the credit that changes everything: “HDM will credit against the purchase price a dollar value representing that portion of the Prepayment Amount corresponding to Prepaid Output yet to be delivered.” On a prepaid deal at year six, that’s roughly fourteen years of prepaid production credited against the price of a six-year-old system.

The battery (ESSA §8(d)): its credit is time-based — the storage prepayment × the fraction of the 10-year term remaining, capped at the battery’s FMV. And note the battery-side FMV must be “determined in good faith by HDM and reasonably acceptable to you” — a reasonableness check written into their own paper. ESSA §8(b): with a concurrent PPA, battery and solar transfer together as one year-6 event.

The floor under all of it: whatever the arithmetic produces, the installer’s written guarantee covers any remaining cost of the transfer — so the math is the mechanism, and the guarantee is the backstop. (Confirm that guarantee appears in your installer agreement — see §1.)

The timeline — mark your calendar, both notices: - HDM sends you a Transfer Notice (FMV + credit figures) no later than 15 days before the sixth anniversary — PPA §10(b). - Solar: your written intent within 60 days after the anniversary; payment (if any) within 30 days of the Transfer Notice — PPA §10(b). - Battery: your purchase notice 30–60 days before the intended purchase date — ESSA §8(c). The battery notice runs on a different clock than the solar one; since both close together, send both notices around the anniversary window and the timing resolves itself.

On transfer (PPA §10(e) / ESSA §8(e)): the agreements terminate — including HDM’s obligations — and “all workmanship and equipment manufacturer warranties included with the Solar System will automatically be assigned to you.” The warranties ride through.

5. If HDM sells the contract, fails, or goes bankrupt

  • HDM may assign the agreement (PPA §12(e)) — like a mortgage being sold; the terms travel unchanged, and §12(f)–(g) require every transfer document to carry the covenant below.
  • The no-removal covenant: neither HDM nor any assignee, successor, or creditor may remove or permanently decommission the system during the term — PPA §12(f)–(g) / ESSA §10(f)–(g). HDM warrants that no partner, owner, or creditor holds such a right.
  • If HDM enters bankruptcy or fails to perform for 90 days, the agreement hands you the trigger: the right to terminate and purchase — PPA §18(a) / ESSA §17(a).
  • And the exit math is pre-agreed: §18(b)’s liquidated-damages clause credits (purchase price − remaining prepayment credit) against what you’d pay — work the algebra and what you owe is the credit you already hold. The structure is designed so the arithmetic, not anyone’s discretion, sets your outcome.

We won’t tell you a bankruptcy would be pleasant — no one can promise what a court does. What we can show is that the contract pre-wires your exit — and the economics point the same way: in a solar financier’s bankruptcy, the operating customer contracts ARE the estate’s value, which is why, in the major cases to date, systems stayed on roofs producing while the paper changed hands.

6. Selling your home

Before year six, the agreement must be assigned to the buyer (PPA §12(a)) — it runs with the title automatically, including through foreclosure. For your buyer, a prepaid agreement is arguably a selling point: power already paid for, $0/month, 0% escalator, a flat $250 assignment fee, no credit check. And §10(a) gives you a purchase window opening 30 days before a sale, so at or after the sixth anniversary you can also simply take ownership and sell the house with owned solar. Your rights pass to heirs (§12(b)).

7. During the term — who maintains, monitors, and insures

  • HDM operates, maintains, monitors, and services the warranties — PPA §14(c) / ESSA §12(b). HDM insures the system — PPA §15(a) / ESSA §13(a).
  • The installer is the hands: your disclosure paperwork (Box F) names the installing contractor as the maintenance and repair provider.
  • You watch everything yourself — the monitoring app is yours throughout; nothing about HDM’s ownership dims your visibility.
  • Your only upkeep obligations: keep trees from shading it (PPA §4(e)), don’t tamper with the meter (§2(d)), and give reasonable access for service (§14(a)–(e)).

8. Performance guarantees

  • Solar, years 1–6: an 85% production guarantee with a refund/credit remedy (PPA §3) — read its exclusions; it’s the industry-standard list. After transfer, the manufacturer’s linear output warranty is yours directly.
  • Battery: guaranteed to retain ≥60% capacity through 10 years or 6,000 cycles (ESSA §3); the remedy — repair, replacement, or paying the market difference — is at HDM’s election, which is standard warranty drafting.
  • What no one guarantees: your exact savings (PPA says so plainly — a rep’s estimate is an estimate) and backup sufficiency in every outage (ESSA §14(c)–(d): a battery is not a whole-home generator; sizing sets expectations).

9. Battery-specific terms worth knowing

  • HDM sets charge/discharge/reserve settings during the term (ESSA §2(a)) — yours at transfer.
  • Broadband connection required to keep warranty coverage (§2(d)).
  • No new generator without written consent; an existing one must be on a separate transfer switch (§2(e)).
  • Do not back up life-critical medical devices (§2(g)) — the standard safety disclaimer in every home-battery contract on the market; keep dedicated medical backup regardless of whose battery you buy.
  • Grid programs (Exhibit B) — read this one as an upside, not a catch. HDM may enroll the battery in utility “Optimization Programs” (demand-response / virtual-power-plant), and the exhibit’s own terms carry the protections: “you will be compensated” (with advance notice of how, including any refund of the Storage Pre-Payment Amount), changes made “without negatively impacting your total energy costs,” and HDM must “ensure the backup reserve for backup power is maintained per your agreement” — your outage protection is contractually held out of the program’s reach. Opt out any time; each opt-out runs a year. Worth knowing: battery owners commonly join these programs on their own because the credits are real — Arizona’s APS program, for example, pays up to $400 per battery per year (tesla.com/support) — and Nevada’s own version just arrived: the PUCN approved NV Energy’s first pay-for-performance grid-services riders on August 11, 2026, paying customer-sited batteries for dispatched energy at hourly market pricing (Utility Dive, Aug 2026), with participation details still being finalized.
  • The battery agreement’s initial term is 10 years (ESSA §11(b)) — shorter than the solar term; one more reason the year-6 combined transfer is the designed path.

10. The legal boilerplate

  • Arbitration (PPA §17 / ESSA §15): individual JAMS arbitration, jury and class waivers — standard in consumer contracts; notably, HDM pays up to $500 of your initiation fee if you file. Worth knowing it’s there; not unique to this agreement.
  • Liability caps and disclaimed implied warranties (PPA §16 / ESSA §14) — the written warranties above are the operative ones.
  • Pre-install changes (PPA §5): if design, production, or tariff assumptions change before installation, HDM may modify or cancel — and you get 5 business days to accept or walk.
  • Cancellation window: the later of 4 business days or 9 calendar days after signing, full deposit refund.
  • Your default (PPA §19): unfixed material breach after 120 days’ notice, your bankruptcy, or abandoning the home — and even then HDM may not remove the system while a financing balance remains.
  • Recorded confirmation (NV law): before installation you confirm on a recorded call that no promises exist outside the written agreements. This is exactly why every promise that matters to you — especially the year-6 transfer guarantee — must be inside a signed agreement, not in an email or a conversation. Ours is. Check that yours is too.

11. Template quirks we’ll flag before you do

Honesty section: things in HDM’s forms we’d fix if we held the pen. - The ESSA names California law (§20(b)) on a Nevada home — a carry-over from HDM’s home state. The PPA doesn’t share the issue. - HDM’s disclosure Box J lists a 5-year roof-penetration warranty and the body says 10 — while the installer’s own agreement carries 25 years, and the installer’s paper is the one that answers for your roof. Whose warranty, which paper: installer’s, 25, in the installation agreement. - Assorted cosmetic template artifacts (a stray merge token, two typos). None operative.

The bottom line

Read as a whole, these agreements sort into three bins: (1) standard financed-equipment terms that dissolve the day you take ownership at year six, (2) the machinery that produces the 30% federal discount and protects it, and (3) ordinary consumer-contract boilerplate. The one promise the HDM paper deliberately doesn’t make — a no-cost transfer — is made in writing by the installer, in the agreement you countersign. Read both.
